Query: How do transformer architectures work in LLMs?
Response:
Transformer architectures have revolutionized the field of natural language processing (NLP) and beyond, with their ability to process sequential data efficiently. In the context of large language models (LLMs), transformers are used as a critical component for text generation and understanding.

The Transformer architecture, introduced in 2017 by Vaswani et al., relies on self-attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of different input elements [1]. This allows the model to capture long-range dependencies in sequential data. The basic Transformer block consists of two linear layers with a feed-forward network (FFN) in between, which helps in learning the contextual relationships between tokens.

In multimodal LLMs, transformers are employed to process and integrate information from multiple modalities, such as text, images, or audio. This enables the model to better understand and generate more coherent output. However, one of the key challenges in multimodal transformer-based LLMs is bridging the modality gap between unimodal networks and unlabeled multimodal data.

To address this challenge, researchers have proposed various techniques, such as knowledge distillation and self-supervised learning. For instance, multimodal knowledge expansion (MKE) [2] proposes a framework for effective utilization of multimodal data without requiring labels. The authors demonstrate that the proposed MKE framework consistently outperforms traditional knowledge distillation methods.

Another key aspect of transformer architectures is their ability to handle sequential data efficiently. In computer vision applications, transformers measure relationships between pairs of input tokens (e.g., words or image parts), leveraging attention mechanisms [3]. While most common visual Transformer architectures rely on the encoder alone for transformation tasks like image classification, other applications have successfully utilized both the decoder and encoder parts.

In the context of self-supervised learning, multimodal learning has emerged as an attractive strategy to alleviate annotation bottlenecks. Self-supervised multimodal learning (SSML) [4] provides a comprehensive review of state-of-the-art methods for learning from raw multimodal data without labels. The authors elucidate three major challenges intrinsic to SSML: representation learning from multimodal data, modality fusion, and unaligned data learning.

In summary, transformer architectures play a crucial role in LLMs, enabling efficient processing of sequential data and effective integration of multiple modalities. However, addressing the modality gap and bridging the challenge of annotation bottlenecks remain significant open problems in this field.
